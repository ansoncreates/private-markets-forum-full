Executive Summary

Private credit has scaled in assets faster than it has scaled in infrastructure. This survey confirms that thesis with data.

We surveyed senior operators across the AUM spectrum — from sub-$1B emerging managers to $50B+ global platforms — with deliberate emphasis on the largest and most operationally complex firms in the market. 42% of respondents manage $50B+ in AUM. These are the platforms where infrastructure failure has the largest consequences, and where the findings carry the most weight.

Across this sample, the industry converges on the same core problem: a unified data model that does not yet exist. LP pressure is rising everywhere. AI adoption is accelerating across the board. But where the infrastructure actually breaks, and how firms respond, depends on where they sit on the growth curve.

This report presents aggregate findings across five pillars: the Scaling Constraints Map, the LP Pressure Index, the Modernization Trajectory, the Build List, and What Breaks at Scale — the segmented view showing how constraints shift from people to process to architecture as platforms scale.

Analysis: The Three Dominant Constraints

01

Cross-system data model / integrations

Cross-system data integration leads the weighted ranking despite not being the most common single #1 choice. Its strength comes from breadth: 53% of respondents placed it in their top three. This is the constraint that touches everything. When firms cannot unify data across portfolio systems, accounting platforms, and reporting tools, every downstream function requires manual intervention to bridge the gaps.

02

Talent (hiring/retaining ops & tech staff)

Talent is the most frequently cited #1 constraint (24%), reflecting an industry where operations and technology roles are chronically understaffed. With 58% of firms reporting investment-weighted staffing ratios of 3:1 or above, the operational side of the house is being asked to do more with less.

03

LP reporting (frequency, granularity, bespoke)

LP reporting rounds out the top three. Notably, it ranks as a #1 or #2 constraint for 32% of respondents. This is not a back-office nuisance. It is a scaling bottleneck that directly affects fundraising capacity and LP retention.

The Reporting Trifecta

Three reporting-related demands dominate the LP pressure landscape, and they reinforce each other. LPs want reports faster, they want them customized to their templates, and they want selfservice access to the underlying data. Meeting any one of these demands is manageable. Meeting all three simultaneously requires a fundamentally different data architecture than most GPs have today.

This is the link between the LP Pressure Index and the Scaling Constraints Map. The LP demands documented here are, in effect, an external forcing function on the same cross-system data integration problem identified as the #1 scaling constraint. GPs cannot deliver faster, bespoke, ondemand reporting without clean, normalized, continuously updated data pipelines.

The interviews revealed that LP pressure operates through multiple mechanisms, not all of which are visible in the survey data. At larger platforms, the pressure is self-reinforcing: competitive positioning creates promises that become operational mandates.

But once you’ve made that promise, the pressure to actually deliver it flows back onto the front office. And then an LP sees another manager offering the same thing, comes to us and says can you do that too. The promises compound.”

— Product & Strategy Leader, $50B+ Platform

A Head of Credit Product at a major technology platform offered a more structural view. In his framing, the reporting pressure is a symptom of a deeper shift in how LPs are asserting themselves:

“LPs are starting to walk with their dollars toward managers who will give them an easier time. At smaller firms, the dynamic is different. An emerging manager building from scratch was direct about how LP pressure actually works in practice. In his telling, institutional LPs do not coach GPs on what to upgrade. They run a red-flag checklist and move on if anything is missing.

“The easiest thing for an LP to do is run their red list. If anyone hits any of those flags, they’re out. Then they sort by returns, by track record, by pedigree, and the top one or two float to the top. They don’t coach you on what to improve. They just move to the next one.”

— CFO, Emerging Manager (Pre-Launch)

A mid-market Managing Director described a third mechanism: competitive benchmarking. Many of his firm’s LPs are also investors in competitor funds. If another manager provides something his firm does not, those LPs will ask for it. The bar keeps rising because someone else already raised it.

One concrete data point from the interviews that deserves specific mention: side letter agreements at some firms now require LP estimates within 10 business days for private credit portfolios. That timeline compresses the entire valuation workflow and puts strain on every upstream process.

From the Interviews: How Operators Think About AI

The interviews surfaced sharper frameworks for AI deployment than the survey data alone can capture. The most pointed came from a strategy leader at a $50B+ platform, who drew a clear line between firms that are succeeding with AI and those that are not.

“The companies that have done successful AI implementation go an inch wide and a mile deep. They fully automate one specific workflow that they’ve already mastered manually. Then you put an agent on it. The mistake is thinking you can just drop in an LLM and it’ll figure things out.”

— Product & Strategy Leader, $50B+ Platform

His framework for the build-versus-buy question in the AI era was equally direct: build what is bespoke and differentiating, buy what someone else has already solved. The interesting shift is that AI has made building so cheap that the old calculus is changing. But the prerequisite for all of it, in his view, is structured data in a warehouse. Without that foundation, AI deployment is premature.

On the human-in-the-loop question, a Managing Director at a mid-market firm was clear about where the line sits. His firm uses AI to process volume at the front end of the deal pipeline, filtering thousands of potential deals down to the ones worth serious attention. But no final investment decision will ever be made purely off AI output. The tool filters and prioritizes. A human being makes the final call every time. That boundary, in his view, is non-negotiable in private credit.

That same respondent was candid about the noise level in the AI vendor market. He receives cold outreach weekly from AI products promising to automate some part of his process. His observation:

if he believed everything he was being pitched, his firm could let AI run the entire business. The challenge is not finding AI products. It is evaluating which ones actually meet the firm’s standards.

From the Interviews: The Data Model as Foundation

The interviews reinforced the unified data model finding with operator experience. A Credit Controller at a firm that built its credit business from scratch within a larger PE platform described attending a Private Funds CFO conference where the question “if you had to start over, what would you do first?” came up repeatedly. The answer was near-universal:

“A unified data model with a data warehouse where all position and accounting data from various sources flows into one place, all the relationships are built, everything speaks to each other. I left that conference thinking: I think we got that one right. It’s probably the hardest thing to undo as you scale, because everything builds on top of it.”

— Credit Controller, PE Platform with Nascent Credit Strategy

A Head of Credit Product at a major technology platform offered the most structural version of the build list answer. His wand wave was not a technology feature but a market structure change: lifecycle activity in private credit (paydowns, amendments, agent notices) is still processed through PDFs that people then scrape with LLMs. His vision is that a paydown should be captured as a digital record at the moment it is created, then disseminated directly to systems of record as a transaction.

“In 2026, in an AI-first era, that makes no sense. The paydown should be captured as a digital record at the moment it’s created. Not: create it digitally, print it to paper, send it as a PDF, and then reconstruct the digital record on the receiving end.”

— Head of Credit Product, Global Technology Platform

His point was that individual firm-level technology improvements will always be capped in their impact if the underlying market rails do not change. That change requires ecosystem participants (technology providers, data providers, agent banks, lenders) to coordinate. It will not happen through individual firm action alone.

We asked: if you could wave a wand and solve one operational problem, what would it be? The responses cluster tightly. The unified data model is the clear winner, cited by nearly half the sample. This is the single strongest signal in the entire survey.

The dominance of the unified data model response (45%) reflects a fundamental architectural deficit. Most private credit platforms operate with fragmented data spread across multiple systems, strategies, and formats. The direct lending team uses one system. The ABF team uses another. The accounting team uses a third. And the reporting team stitches them together manually.

Until this problem is solved, every downstream function—reporting, risk, compliance, monitoring— will continue to require manual intervention to bridge the gaps. This is why cross-system integration appears as the #1 weighted scaling constraint, the #3 manual workaround area, and the dominant “wand” answer. The industry is describing the same problem from three different angles.

The top scaling constraints shift markedly across firm sizes. Each tier faces a fundamentally different version of the infrastructure challenge.

Conclusion: The Infrastructure Imperative

The survey data paints a consistent picture: private credit's infrastructure is not keeping pace with its ambition. The industry is growing at 1.5–2x. LP demands are intensifying. Multi-strategy complexity is compounding. And the operational systems that underpin most platforms were built for a smaller, simpler era.

The good news is that the industry knows it. Technology budgets are rising. AI is moving from exploration to production. Data platforms are being built. But the core architectural challenge— creating a unified, normalized data layer that can serve multiple strategies, multiple investor types, and multiple reporting demands simultaneously—remains the industry’s most critical unsolved problem.

The firms that solve this first will be the platforms that scale. The vendors that build to this need will find a market starving for solutions. And the industry standards that emerge—common identifiers, standardized loan tapes, interoperable APIs—will be the foundation on which the next generation of private credit infrastructure is built.

Appendix: Methodology

Survey Design

The 2026 Private Credit Infrastructure Survey was designed as a structured, quantitative instrument targeting operational and technology leaders at private credit managers. The survey covers firm context, scaling constraints, manual workaround concentration, LP pressure drivers, modernization trajectory, AI adoption, and open-ended gap identification.

Question design prioritized forced-ranking approaches over “select all that apply” formats for prioritization questions, enabling weighted scoring analysis that reveals true prioritization rather than general acknowledgment. All ranking questions use a top-3 format with explicit ordinal selection.

Data Collection

The survey was deployed via Typeform and distributed through The Private Markets Forum’s network of private credit operators, with targeted outreach to COOs, CTOs, CIOs, Heads of Data, and other operational and technology leadership. Survey design was stress-tested with senior industry operators prior to launch. This report reflects responses from 38 senior operators collected between January and March 2026. The sample was built through targeted outreach — not broad distribution — with the explicit goal of reaching the decision-makers at the largest and most operationally complex platforms in the market. 42% of respondents manage $50B+ in private credit assets. Findings are further enriched by follow-up qualitative interviews with select operators conducted while the survey was still in the field. These interviews inform the narrative analysis throughout this report.

Analysis Approach

Ranking questions are analyzed using weighted scoring (3 points for #1 selection, 2 for #2, 1 for #3) to surface true prioritization. Multi-select and categorical questions are reported as simple frequency distributions. Open-ended responses are thematically coded and reported as aggregated categories with representative anonymized quotations. All data is aggregated and anonymized. No individual firm or respondent is identified in this report.
